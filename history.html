History & Timeline of Maze Search Algorithms

Maze searching algorithms evolved from 19th-century manual puzzle-solving heuristics into foundational graph traversal techniques powering modern computer science, network routing, and robotics.

Timeline of Key Milestones:

19th Century (1882) — Depth-First Search (DFS) Roots:
French engineer Charles Pierre Trémaux designed Trémaux's algorithm, an early manual maze-solving method involving drawing chalk marks on pathways to guarantee an exit without cycling. In 1972, John Hopcroft and Robert Tarjan formally adapted and analyzed DFS for computer algorithms.

1945 & 1959 — Breadth-First Search (BFS):
First formulated theoretically in 1945 by German computing pioneer Konrad Zuse in his thesis on the Plankalkül programming language. In 1959, Edward F. Moore independently reinvented BFS to compute shortest paths through telephone switching circuits and mazes, further popularized by C. Y. Lee in 1961 for automated circuit board wire routing.

1968 — A* Search Algorithm:
Developed at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) by Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson, and Bertram Raphael for Shakey the Robot—the first general-purpose mobile robot capable of reasoning about its own actions. By extending Edsger Dijkstra's 1959 shortest path algorithm with heuristic distance estimations, A* allowed the robot to find optimal paths exponentially faster.
